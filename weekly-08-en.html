The Permit Grants a Million Satellites — Cooling Them Was Ruled Out of Scope

The FCC did not decide whether orbital data centers work. It declared that question outside its authority and left the rest to a 14-year runway, a deferred disposal waiver, a $170 billion-per-gigawatt estimate, and a radiator nobody has yet built

On 25 November the Space Bureau of the US Federal Communications Commission disposed of SpaceX's orbital data center application in an order numbered DA 26-1273. The disposition is "GRANTED-IN-PART / DEFERRED-IN-PART — With Conditions." The grant covers up to one million non-geostationary satellites, in six shells each 50 km thick at altitudes from 500 to 1,002 km, at 30-degree and sun-synchronous inclinations. The frequencies are 18.8–19.3 GHz (space-to-Earth) and 28.6–29.1 GHz (Earth-to-space) in the Ka-band.

The most important sentence in the fourteen pages is not the grant. It is Condition 4. SpaceX had asked the Commission to waive the rule requiring satellites ending their mission below 2,000 km to be disposed of by atmospheric re-entry, so that satellites above 600 km could be parked in Earth disposal orbits or heliocentric orbits. The Commission deferred the request. Its reason is one sentence: SpaceX "has not provided sufficient information for the Commission to determine whether its alternative disposal methods are compliant with the Commission's requirements."

What that deferral means in practice is heavier than the table suggests. Condition 11 finds that the debris mitigation plan for satellites operating below 600 km complies with the rules, and conditions the grant on a modification application carrying a final plan before any satellite operates above 600 km. Read the two together: of the six authorized shells, two are usable now — 550–568 km and 565–585 km. The other four, at 686–718 km, 707–744 km, 946–978 km and 967–1,002 km, stay shut until SpaceX files more paperwork.

And one more thing. What this licence actually regulates is a backup radio. Starmind's primary communications are optical inter-satellite links feeding results into the Starlink constellation. The Ka-band the FCC authorized is for backup and emergency telemetry, tracking and command when those optical links fail. The business itself — the compute, the heat, the optical mesh — is not the subject of this licence. The order says so itself.

What the Commission licensed was not a million units of compute. It was the emergency channel through which a million units may talk to Earth. Every other question was dismissed as beyond its authority.

Chairman Brendan Carr's statement frames the decision this way: "America is leading the world into the era of super intelligence… This action opens a bold new frontier for the computing infrastructure needed to power America's leadership and growth." The press release goes further, saying the grant "promises to bring the human race closer to a Kardashev II civilization — one that can harness the full power of the sun."

So this article asks one question. Given a fourteen-year runway, can physics and economics fill the permit inside it? The FCC did not answer. Not answering is the content of the decision.

What the permit actually did: it slowed the clock and removed the bond

On the numbers alone this is the largest space licence ever issued. On structure, three things happened. First, a million satellites were authorized. Second, the clock that required them to be launched was pushed back by seven years. Third, the surety bond that prices failure was waived.

Under the old §25.164, a licensee had to deploy 50% within six years of grant and the rest within nine. SpaceX initially asked for a complete waiver of those milestones. On 19 October it changed its request: instead of a waiver, apply the new part 100 schedule the Commission adopted in its July Space Modernization proceeding. The Commission agreed. The result is 2033, 2035, 2038 and 2040.

The swap matters more than the arithmetic. Under the new schedule, what is required by November 2033 is one satellite. The condition for keeping a million-satellite licence is that a single satellite is operating seven years from now. Nothing needs to fly before then. And the fifteen-year term starts not at the grant but on the day SpaceX certifies that the first satellite is operating in orbit. If that satellite flies in 2028, the licence runs to 2043. The runway is longer than fourteen years.

The bond waiver points the same way. The §25.165 bond exists to stop operators from warehousing orbital resources they never intend to use. The Commission's stated ground is that the spectrum use is limited to backup, non-interference operations, so "there is little public interest benefit to rigid application of the surety bond requirement." The logic is coherent: if you have licensed only an emergency radio, there is no reason to bond the emergency radio.

The shadow of that coherence is the point. The bond and the milestones were the instruments that measured progress. A licence without them is a licence that does not measure progress. Amazon Leo put the objection precisely in its March petition: "SpaceX seeks a waiver of any rule that might hold it accountable for deploying a million satellites on any specific timeline… The Application seems to describe a lofty ambition rather than a real plan — and a speculative placeholder rather than a complete application." The Commission denied the petition, writing that because it had applied deployment milestones, the concern was moot.

One thing should be said plainly. This is not a careless licence. The conditions are dense: coordination with federal systems, a 24/7 interference contact two weeks before any use of the downlink band, five categories of semi-annual reporting, a modification application before the high shells. As an administrative instrument it is careful about radio interference and orbital debris — and not about whether the business exists.

The line the FCC drew for itself: environment, astronomy, feasibility

The most-quoted passage will be ¶14. Citing its October NEPA order (FCC 26-64), the Commission declined to require further environmental review of Starmind. The theory is that Commission-licensed space-based operations are "extraterritorial activities" and therefore not a major Federal action: space is not US territory, the United States is not sovereign over it, and the licence does not create federal control or responsibility over what follows.

The timing matters. That rule was published in the Federal Register on 17 November and, for most provisions, takes effect thirty days later — 17 December. The Starmind grant came before that. The Commission knew, and wrote: the new rules "do not come into effect until 30 days after publication… but even applying our current rule framework, we decline to require further environmental review under NEPA." A second, independent basis was laid down in case the first is unavailable.

The details fall into place. Launch emissions are "outside of the Commission's purview," with the FAA responsible for launch environmental review. Atmospheric effects of satellites burning up on re-entry "remain speculative and not supported in the record." Optical astronomy is sufficiently addressed by SpaceX's commitment to coordinate with the National Science Foundation. The thermal signature raised by the AAS is a "non-spectrum related impact… outside the scope of the ODC's radiofrequency operations." Orbital congestion is dismissed because the rules set no limit on the number of satellites authorized with similar orbital parameters, and the Bureau "declines to invent a de facto rule in this adjudicatory licensing proceeding."

Then comes the paragraph that tells you what kind of document this is. To commenters who doubted that orbital data centers are feasible, efficient, or necessary, the Commission says: "In many cases, the comments seek to substitute the government's judgment regarding the viability of business endeavors for that of the applicant and private enterprise. We decline to do so."

The regulator announced it would not ask whether the thing works. Instead it set, very generously, when and how many. Both sentences are needed to understand the decision.

Physics I: heat arrives first

Of everything the FCC declined to examine, heat answers first. There is no convection in vacuum. A data centre's waste heat leaves only by radiation. Under Stefan–Boltzmann, emitted power scales with area and with the fourth power of absolute temperature. If you cannot raise the temperature, you enlarge the area — and area is mass, volume and launch cost.

IEEE Spectrum's June calculation shows the size of the problem. Keeping a single 700 W H100 at 60 °C requires about 1.4 m² of radiator. A rack of 32 GPUs — roughly 40 kW with CPUs, memory and networking — needs about 80 m², half a pickleball court. A 100 MW facility needs 2,500 of those racks. And ultraviolet light and atomic oxygen degrade radiator coatings in low orbit, so after five years the same heat needs 40% more area.

Academic models point the same way. An April analysis (arXiv 2604.27197) puts a 1 MW orbital node at 5,640 m² of photovoltaics, 2,500 m² of radiator, and 29.4 kg/kW for power, storage and heat rejection — 34 to 59 kg/kW once the spacecraft is included. The same paper notes that at that mass, matching a terrestrial benchmark of $10–40 per watt leaves $250–1,000 per kilogram for launch plus spacecraft build combined. Falcon 9's public dedicated launch price alone is 3.4 to 13.5 times above that allowance.

SpaceX's published first-generation satellite, AI1, aims straight at that arithmetic: 30 m deployed height, 75 m wingspan, compute payload up to 250 kW peak and 175 kW average, at 75 kW per tonne. The company says radiating into vacuum "reduces cooling power overhead by an order of magnitude" against ground cooling. At 75 kW per tonne, a satellite weighs about 2.3 tonnes on average power or 3.3 tonnes at peak. The filing's envelope is under 4 tonnes.

Multiply a million satellites by 175 kW and you get 175 GW. That is about 103 times the 1.7 GW of nameplate compute SpaceX reported on 30 September. Part of why the number looks enormous is that the denominator is small. What is required by 2033 is one satellite, and the question is how large its radiator must be.

Two government documents reach the same conclusion. The GAO wrote in April that large data centers will need solar arrays "larger than any launched and assembled in space as of April 2026" and that cooling at that scale is unproven. The ICEF's November roadmap is blunter: "Thermal management is one of the most fundamental determinants of both feasibility and cost… the size and mass of the radiators — and the temperature at which the chips are allowed to run — largely set how much compute a satellite can carry." It adds that the weights claimed by leading developers "appear to be highly optimistic and beyond currently-proven technology."

Physics II: mass sets the schedule

If heat sets the ceiling per satellite, mass sets the schedule for the enterprise. A million satellites at four tonnes is four million tonnes. Consider SpaceX's own disclosures. Mass to orbit was 476 tonnes in the third quarter and 1,517 tonnes over nine months. At that rate, four million tonnes takes 8,400 quarters — about 2,100 years.

Starship does change the equation. There were two Starship launches in the third quarter; one was Flight 14, the first orbital flight, which carried 26 Starlink V3 satellites and, by the company's account, added roughly ten times the downlink capacity of a Falcon 9 Starlink launch. The problem is scale. SpaceX's S-1 states the target plainly: "Deployment of 100 gigawatts per year via satellites carrying over 100 kilowatts of compute power per metric ton will require thousands of launches per year and the transport of approximately one million metric tons to orbit annually." The company's own framing is a million tonnes a year. Four million tonnes is four years of that.

Outside estimates are more conservative. The ICEF calculates that sustaining a fleet with 10% of projected 2030 AI data center demand would need "at least 700 launches of Starship-sized rockets per year (2 launches per day) in perpetuity." Amazon's petition came at the same wall from another side: 4,526 satellites were launched worldwide in 2025, so one million would take more than 220 years; with a five-year life, steady state requires replacing 200,000 a year, 44 times the entire global output of 2025.

None of this has to be read as proof of impossibility. If Starship genuinely flies 700 times a year, the arithmetic changes. But 700 has never been achieved, and the third quarter of 2026 saw two Starship launches. Mass is set by physics and production capacity, not by regulation, and it is the one variable that cannot be deferred.

Economics: $170 billion per gigawatt, and no customers yet

Wood Mackenzie estimated a hypothetical 1 GW orbital data center at about $170 billion. That is more than three times a terrestrial facility, and most of the gap is the cost of lifting it off Earth. The firm concluded launch costs would need to fall roughly 70% to compete.

SpaceX's own pace shows where this sits inside the company. Third-quarter revenue was $14.6 billion, up 161%; net income $4.4 billion; adjusted EBITDA $8.8 billion. Capital expenditure in the same quarter was $19.6 billion — more than revenue — of which $16.4 billion went to the Super Intelligence segment. Nine-month capex was $48 billion. Guidance for the fourth quarter is $35–40 billion. Backlog stood at $63 billion.

Most of that money goes to data centers on the ground. Nameplate compute was 1.7 GW at the end of September, with 2.5–3 GW targeted by year-end. Starmind sits beside that as an option. The S-1 defines it — "we expect to begin deploying our orbital AI compute satellites as early as 2028" — and then, in the risk factors, says of its initiatives: they "have not yet been proven at commercial scale, or at all, and may ultimately be unsuccessful."

The market's reaction was muted. On 25 November SPCX fell as much as 9% intraday before closing 1.3% higher at $127.44. The S&P 500 fell 1.6% the same day and Nvidia dropped 7.4%. The licence was not a repricing of orbital compute; it was a headline partly offset by fears about funding AI. By 27 November the stock was $131.69, up 3.3%. What moves it is the direction of rates, not the feasibility of the business.

And there are no customers yet. SpaceX has not named a buyer for orbital capacity; its AI revenue today comes from data centers on Earth. The licence is not revenue. It is a call option, and the option's value is set not by its strike but by the volatility of its underlying — which here is radiators and launch mass.

Law: 25 December, and the deferred disposal waiver

Two gates follow. One administrative, one judicial. Under 47 CFR §1.115, a delegated Bureau decision may be brought before the full Commission within thirty days of public notice. Based on the 25 November release, that window closes around 25 December. SpaceX separately has thirty days to decline the grant as conditioned. Only after that does a federal court of appeals open.

The litigation has been signalled. On 8 July, Earthjustice, with Dark Sky International and others, petitioned the FCC to pause orbital data center applications pending a programmatic environmental impact statement. Its senior attorney Jan Hasselman said: "If we have to sue so that they comply, we will." After the grant he called the decision reckless. As of 27 November, no complaint had been reported as filed. That is still a threat, not a case.

The weakest legal point is not the extraterritorial theory in the abstract but its application. In Seven County (2025) the Supreme Court held that NEPA addresses the effects of the project under review rather than an open-ended chain of upstream and downstream consequences, while making clear that an effect occurring at a distance is not automatically outside the analysis. Aluminium oxide from re-entering satellites settling in the stratosphere, and reflected light over American territory, are not effects that occur only outside the United States. That is precisely where the Commission wrote "speculative." It is also a rule that had not yet taken effect when the licence issued.

In practice, though, the next gate is not the courthouse. It is the Condition 4 modification. To use the four shells above 600 km, SpaceX must file a disposal plan, and the Commission will then rule — for the first time — on disposing of satellites somewhere other than Earth's atmosphere. If heliocentric disposal is allowed, that judgment becomes the precedent for every high-altitude constellation that follows. If it is refused, more than half the constellation has nowhere to go. The deferral is not an escape; it is a scheduled next round.

Astronomy has already recorded the decision as a regulatory failure. The AAS said it was "disheartened and dismayed," warning that "the waste heat generated by the data centers would produce a thermal signature that would increase the infrared background of the sky" and noting "the lack of formal coordination requirements in the license." ESO said the approval "highlights the urgent need for international mechanisms capable of assessing activities whose impacts extend far beyond national borders." Neither is a party to a suit. Both are on the record.

Competition: why the same band went to everyone

Read this as an exclusive franchise and you miss the structure. The FCC is reviewing the same Ka-band, on the same terms, for competitors. Blue Origin has filed for 51,600 satellites under Project Sunrise, at 500–1,800 km and 97–104 degrees. Starcloud has filed for 88,000 at 600–850 km. The Commission's press release says it is reviewing the other proposals and "intends to issue decisions on each expeditiously."

So the value of this licence is not spectrum exclusivity. Having waived the processing rounds, SpaceX holds no priority; it must instead protect earlier and later operators and coordinate with them. The order uses exactly that fact to argue it is not spectrum warehousing. What remains is a starting gun and a benchmark. Once a ceiling of one million has been granted, every later review is measured against Starmind: Blue Origin's 51,600 and Starcloud's 88,000 clear a bar that has already been set very high. Carr's remark that "competitor constellations will use solar power" is not a concession; it is the policy. The licence is for an industry, not a company.

What is genuinely scarce, then, is not spectrum but launch mass, radiators, and space-qualified AI silicon. SpaceX said on 24 August that the first Starmind generation will use an optimized Nvidia Vera Rubin NVL72 system. Its Gigasat Factory in Bastrop, Texas, is described as enabling "thousands of AI satellites starting as soon as late 2027." None of those three bottlenecks was before the Commission. Regulation handed out the entry ticket. The race is on another track.

Korea: entering through components, not platforms

Korea's answer is already on paper. The government made a space data center one of its K-Moonshot missions and presented a three-stage roadmap at a National Assembly forum on 2 November. In November 2028 a 100 kg demonstration satellite is to ride as a secondary payload on the seventh Nuri launch, testing a domestic NPU compute module, deployed solar arrays and loop heat-pipe cooling in a dawn-dusk sun-synchronous orbit at about 561 km for more than a year. The project is budgeted at KRW 14 billion, including about KRW 2 billion for the launch. A 500 kg platform in 2031 is to demonstrate an actual AI service, with a multi-tonne platform in 2034.

The strategic choice is not modesty, it is positioning. The K-Moonshot task force states that competing at the same level as global big tech platforms "has no practical benefit," and has chosen instead to place Korean AI chips, power, thermal control and optical communications into the global supply chain. Seventeen companies attended the August KASA meeting, among them Rebellions, FuriosaAI, DeepX, Mobilint, SK hynix, LG Energy Solution and Hanwha Solutions.

That choice maps exactly onto the bottleneck list in the FCC order: heat rejection, power, radiation-tolerant compute, optical links. If Starmind moves toward 175 GW, those four become the most expensive parts. The constraints are budget and heritage. The 2027 budget proposal contains KRW 2.1 billion for core space data center technology and nothing for the demonstration satellite; to make the 2028 launch, design work must begin in January 2027. KARI's emphasis was on "space heritage": a part that performs well on the ground is not adopted without orbital operating history, and without adoption no history accumulates.

The comparison is uncomfortable but necessary. The United States is arguing about a licence for an industry estimated at $170 billion per gigawatt. Korea is arguing about the budget for a KRW 14 billion test satellite. That gap is about scale, not direction. But for the direction to hold, design has to start in January 2027. Starmind's fourteen-year runway and K-Moonshot's twenty-four-month development schedule sit on the same calendar.

Signal assessment — five markers met
Structural change · US regulation has moved from "who launches what" to "who computes what." A satellite licence now sets the ground for compute infrastructure, and the decision pushed environment, astronomy and feasibility outside its own authority. This is the first case in which the licensing standard for space infrastructure shifted from interference to industrial policy.
Money and power · A million satellites and the Ka-band were issued as an option. The cost is a filing and one satellite by 2033; the benefit is fourteen years of financeability and a benchmark. The power to judge moves from the FCC to radiators, launch mass and chip supply — that is, back to industry.
Precedent · The first orbital data center constellation licence. The first combination that waives processing rounds, bond and milestones together while deferring only disposal. Blue Origin's 51,600 and Starcloud's 88,000 will be reviewed against it.
Domain crossing · Space (orbits, debris) × energy (heat rejection, power) × regulation (spectrum, environment) × capital (capex, valuation) × astronomy × international law (extraterritoriality, ITU) all meet in one document. Korea's K-Moonshot and its semiconductor supply chain are another face of the same event.
Contention · A grant issued before the NEPA rule took effect (17 December), the reach of the extraterritorial theory, the precedent of heliocentric disposal, four locked shells above 600 km, and the physical premise of 700 launches a year all hang together.

What to watch

① 17 December and 25 December. The NEPA rule's effective date and the rehearing deadline sit ten days apart. Watch whether Amazon, Viasat or the AAS file under §1.115, whether Earthjustice goes to court, and how the Commission defends applying a rule that was not yet in force.

② The above-600 km modification. It is both the key to four shells and the main event in any litigation. Watch the filing date, whether heliocentric disposal is allowed, and whether the array and radiator envelope is fixed differently from the application.

③ Q3 2027, late 2027 and 2028. The company has pointed to a first Starmind launch on Starship in the third quarter of 2027, volume production at Bastrop as soon as late 2027, and deployment from 2028. Watch Starship cadence (two in Q3), mass to orbit (476 tonnes in Q3), and the first satellite's actual orbit and thermal test results.

④ The direction of capex. Of the $35–40 billion guided for the fourth quarter, how much goes to orbit. Watch the SI capex breakdown, whether orbital capacity contracts appear in the $63 billion backlog, and whether a first customer is named.

⑤ Rival applications. Same band, same terms, same queue. Watch when and on what conditions Blue Origin's 51,600 and Starcloud's 88,000 are decided, and whether their disposal and debris conditions match Starmind's.

⑥ Korea's 2027 budget. Watch whether the KRW 2.1 billion for core technology grows to include the roughly KRW 12 billion demonstration satellite, and whether design work actually starts in January 2027.

One line

The FCC did not judge whether orbital data centers work. It declared it would not judge, and instead slowed the clock and removed the bond. The remaining examiners are the radiator and the launch mass, and neither grants deferrals.
